Auditing GenAI through Lesbian Film Data

The Representations of our Representations will be our Representations

About This Project

Three Degrees of Lesbian Representation
Or: Looking at AI-generated Lesbian film data and wondering what will happen to women loving women when the algorithm makes the call and the representations of our representations become our new representations.

Read more...

Since the arrival of the image-generation model Dall-E, I've been fascinated by assessing its default responses. Why, for example, in early requests, when working with DALL-E 2, did requests for the prompt "Protest" repeatedly produce an image of a Frankensteined white, young man in a red leather jacket? That particular set of outputs, as a colleague noted, seems to rely on James Dean's Rebel Without a Cause aesthetic as a starting point.

And why did early outputs for "Show me a Lesbian" produce equally Frankensteined women showing visible traces of Sandra Bernhard? And why, in later models — Dall-E 3 and 4 — does the Frankensteinian quality give way to a doll-like smoothness, and show rainbow flags draped everywhere to make Queerness loudly legible? In what ways does this algorithmically rendered Queerness exist in relation to a heteronormative default?

Recent AI-related developments in the entertainment sector (AI video slop, Writers Guild and SAG strikes, and Tilly Norwood) prompted me to consider how the use of GenAI models as collaborators in the creation of future cultural artefacts could influence Lesbian representation. To assess this, I designed an audit experiment to observe how ChatGPT retrieves and remixes data from existing Lesbian films. Following the basic tenets of auditing — "repeatedly and systematically querying an algorithm with inputs and observing the corresponding outputs in order to draw inferences about its opaque inner workings" (Metaxa et al) — I prompted ChatGPT-5 to create a list of 50 Lesbian films. I asked the same question 25 times in individual, separate chats. In a dataset of 1256 film titles (sometimes I received more than 50 in a list), 17 films received over 20 mentions.

As the model ventured to pull titles from the deeper well of Queer screen presentations, Lesbian subplots made the cut, subtext alone was deemed good enough, too, and baffling single mentions found their way into the dataset. One of my favorite inclusions was "The Fall of the Roman Empire (fictional) — just kidding, that's not one," which the model pulled from Reddit, including the annotation.

Working further with the 17 most-mentioned film titles, I played a small game of algorithmic telephone. I asked the model to generate stills "inspired by the Lesbian film [title]" and observed that, by and large, the model initially preserved and exaggerated aspects of cultural context and retained the racial identity of the protagonists, while depictions of gender identity moved toward a more feminine, younger look.

I then asked the model to generate a logline from the stills. Comparing the generated loglines to the loglines excerpted from IMDb, I noticed a pronounced shift in language. The word "Lesbian" had disappeared from loglines, and other nouns signifying identity receded in favor of vague formulations of a movie's central dilemma, which places protagonists in a position to "choose between being true to themselves" and some version of duty. Ostensibly, these loglines still hint at Queer stories, but they no longer use clear language to name themselves, thereby producing new ambiguity.

In the next step, I asked the models to create film posters including titles based on the loglines it generated. In my request, I preserved the word "Lesbian." The resulting poster set shows evidence of a center of gravity reminiscent of mass-market romance. And while that's a genre I am fond of, the loss of variety, both aesthetically and verbally, is truly staggering. All titles bearing character names have been replaced with combinations of prepositions and weather conditions or nondescript locations. And so Carol transforms into Through the Rain, and Pariah morphs into Bedroom Sonnets. Aesthetically, the default posters the models generated reminded me of cheap paperback covers. Not a single version generated by GPT-5 offered a more experimental composition.

Throughout the experiment, I had carefully maintained the word "Lesbian" in my generation requests. Perhaps I intuited the compulsive heterosexuality of the default? In the two final rounds of my experiment, I tested the model's response to prompts requesting stills based on its loglines, omitting "Lesbian": GPT-5 turned 6 of 17 into straight stories, left 3 ambiguous, and retained only those that clearly used terms like "two women" in the logline as potentially Queer.

Finally, I asked it to create posters based on the film titles it had generated previously, and I omitted the "Lesbian" from my request: all 17 posters turned un-queer and more racially homogeneous. Not a Lesbian trace was left.

The omissions are what's interesting. The model doesn't know what it doesn't know and has no sense of what it fails to produce. The homogeneity the algorithm continually manufactures becomes the de facto representation, and when the possibility of Queerness has to share algorithmically generated outcomes with the algorithms' defaults, Queerness and Lesbians will disappear from the cultural landscape. The compulsively conservative gender dynamics will help reentrench a norm that sees variety as deviant.

And output by output, it will make deviants of us all.

Maria Baker, March 2026
